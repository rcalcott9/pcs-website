Pacing and return to activity

AboutTreatments (4)

Do a steady amount each day. Build up in small steps.

This page sums up published research reviews and guidelines. It is not a recommendation. Talk to a clinician before you start any treatment.

Order: stronger evidence first, then lower risk, then lower cost. Every treatment we have for this area is listed.

  1. Limiting screen time in the first days after concussion

    Evidence: Limited evidence (Limited trial evidence). Risk: Minimal. Typical cost: Under $100.

    Studied in: One trial in teens and young adults (12-25) seen within a day of injury.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Behavioural advice to cut back on phones, computers and TV for about 2 days. No physical harm is plausible; the main downside is social isolation or missed schoolwork if screen limits are made strict or long.

    Cost: Advice only, given as part of routine concussion care; no extra visits, devices or products. In the trial, the intervention was simply an instruction to avoid screens for 48 hours.

    Sources: Macnow, 2021, JAMA Pediatrics (RCT); Leddy, 2023, Br J Sports Med (Amsterdam 2022 consensus review); Davis, 2024, Pediatrics (Amsterdam 2022 consensus, paediatric recommendations)

  2. Early return to light activity (instead of prolonged rest)

    Evidence: Limited evidence (Limited trial evidence). Risk: Low. Typical cost: Under $100.

    Studied in: Mostly teens after sport concussion, in the first days and weeks.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: The advice is light everyday physical and cognitive activity kept below the level that clearly worsens symptoms, not contact sport or high-intensity exercise. The Amsterdam-informing review concluded that early return to light physical activity in the first 2 days 'safely facilitate[s] recovery' (PMID 37316185). The AAPM&R panel found harm from strict rest and from high-intensity activity, not from light activity, and judged most interventions safe when used judiciously (PMID 37794736). Brief, self-limiting symptom flare-ups with exertion are common, so 'low' rather than 'minimal'. No serious harm specific to light activity was found in these sources, so none is listed. Early return to contact or high-risk activity, with the risk of another head injury, is outside this scope.

    Cost: Course assumption: this is advice given at the usual concussion assessment, then self-managed walking, light daily activity and a gradual return to school or work. It needs no equipment, supervised sessions or extra visits, so the added out-of-pocket cost is about $0. The visit where the advice is given is the standard diagnostic visit and is not counted. Prescribed or supervised aerobic exercise programmes cost more and are listed separately.

    Sources: De Luigi AJ, 2023, PM R (AAPM&R consensus statement with systematic review); Leddy JJ, 2023, Br J Sports Med (systematic review and meta-analysis for the Amsterdam 2022 consensus)

  3. Education and reassurance about recovery

    Evidence: Very little or conflicting evidence. Risk: Minimal. Typical cost: Under $100.

    Studied in: Adults seen in the emergency department soon after injury, and some studies in children and teens.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Education is written or verbal information about expected symptoms and recovery (information sheets, with or without telephone or in-person follow-up). No plausible mechanism for serious physical harm; none of the reviewed trials or reviews report adverse effects. The main theoretical downside is that poorly framed information could heighten symptom worry, which is why guidelines emphasise reassurance about a good expected recovery.

    Cost: Course: an information sheet or brief verbal explanation given at the emergency department or clinic visit (the interventions in the Eliyahu 2016 review), optionally with a follow-up phone call. This is delivered as part of a visit the patient is already having, and patient leaflets are typically free; no separate charge is expected beyond the index visit.

    Sources: Eliyahu, 2016, Acad Emerg Med; Teo, 2020, Brain Inj; Silverberg, 2025, Ann Fam Med (Action Collaborative on TBI Care guideline)

  4. Multidisciplinary or interdisciplinary rehabilitation

    Evidence: Very little or conflicting evidence (“Very low and low levels of evidence” (source wording); weak recommendation). Risk: Minimal. Typical cost: $100 to $1,000.

    Studied in: Adults with lasting post-concussion symptoms.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Coordinated programmes combine education, CBT-based psychological support, graded activity/exercise and therapy from several professions; no drugs, devices or procedures. In the GAIN trials (reused cost/risk profile MULTI-GAIN), the only harm recorded was minor, temporary fatigue or symptom increase (7/148 adults), with no serious adverse events; the adolescent trial reported none. Harms data are thin (two trials, passive collection in one). No sourced serious harm was found.

    Cost: Reused from the MULTI-GAIN profile. Course assumption: a trial-style programme of 1 evaluation, about 3 group and 5 individual sessions over 8 weeks. At 2026 Medicare national non-facility rates (CPT 97162 about $98; 97110 about $29 per 15-minute unit; 90853 about $30 per group session), the total is roughly $700. Self-pay rates are usually higher than Medicare (an unsourced assumption), and broader US multidisciplinary clinic programmes with more sessions or neuropsychological testing can exceed $1,000, so the estimate sits near the upper boundary.

    Sources: Rytter, 2021, JAMA Netw Open (Danish national guideline, GRADE); Möller, 2021, J Rehabil Med; Moore, 2024, Disabil Rehabil
