About balance and dizziness

Treatments for balance and dizziness

Feeling dizzy, off balance, or unsteady.

This page sums up published research reviews and guidelines. It is not a recommendation. Talk to a clinician before you start any treatment.

Order: stronger evidence first, then lower risk, then lower cost. Every treatment we have for this area is listed.

  1. Vestibular rehabilitation

    Evidence: Limited evidence (Low certainty (source rating)). Risk: Low. Typical cost: $100 to $1,000.

    Studied in: Teens and adults with dizziness lasting more than 10 days, and adults with lasting symptoms.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: No concussion trial or review abstract reports serious harms. Outside concussion, the Cochrane review of vestibular rehabilitation for peripheral vestibular dysfunction (39 RCTs) found no reported adverse effects and called it safe (PMID 25581507). The APTA vestibular hypofunction guideline (PMID 34864777) describes transient symptom provocation (dizziness, nausea) as common and expected, since habituation works by provoking symptoms, and notes a possible risk of falls or near-falls during balance exercises, without a rate. Sessions are therapist-supervised. Common minor, self-limiting flare-ups make this low rather than minimal.

    Cost: Course assumption: one PT evaluation plus 16 group vestibular rehabilitation sessions (twice weekly for 8 weeks, as in a trial, PMC8814830). CMS 2026 Physician Fee Schedule national rates: 97162 evaluation $97.86; 97150 group therapy $18.04 per session, so about $390 at Medicare rates and about $770 at twice Medicare, inside the band. Borderline: one-to-one delivery (typical in the US, e.g. 2 x 97112 + 1 x 97110 per 45-minute session) for the same 16 sessions is about $1,610 even at Medicare rates, which is over $1,000. No US cash-price cost analysis was found, so the uninsured figure is estimated from the fee schedule. Insurance usually covers it as outpatient physical therapy.

    Sources: Aljabri, 2024, J Head Trauma Rehabil (systematic review and meta-analysis, 6 RCTs in meta-analysis); Schneider, 2023, Br J Sports Med (systematic review for the Amsterdam 2022 consensus on sport-related concussion); Langhorn, 2021, JAMA Netw Open (Danish systematic review and guideline for persistent post-concussion symptoms)

  2. Neck (cervical) physical therapy and manual therapy

    Evidence: Limited evidence (Limited trial evidence). Risk: Low. Typical cost: Over $1,000.

    • Typical cost over $1,000 without insurance

    Studied in: Teens and adults with neck pain, dizziness or headache lasting more than 10 days.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Minor, short-lived effects such as soreness, stiffness or headache after manual therapy are common: about 22% in RCTs and 41% in cohort studies, with major adverse events about 0.13% (Carnes 2010, PMID 20097115). The recognised serious harm of hands-on neck treatment is cervical artery dissection or stroke. It is very rare, and in a review of 334 published serious cases, 75% followed high-velocity manipulation rather than gentle mobilisation or exercise (Leung 2025, PMID 39663097). Serious harm is rare, so the risk is low for mobilisation and neck exercise. Thrust manipulation calls for vascular screening.

    Cost: Assumes a course of an initial evaluation plus about 6-8 physical therapy visits over 6-8 weeks, similar to cervicovestibular rehabilitation trials. Without insurance, US physical therapy typically costs $100-350+ per session (Hinge Health cost summary), so a typical course costs about $800-2,800. Most courses land above $1,000, though a short course at a low-cost clinic could come in under it. Insurance and Medicare usually cover PT, subject to copays or deductibles.

    Sources: Reid, 2022, Br J Sports Med; Schneider, 2023, Br J Sports Med (Amsterdam 2022 consensus review); Rytter, 2021, JAMA Netw Open (Danish guideline)
