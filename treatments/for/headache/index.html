Headache and migraine

AboutTreatments (15)

Headaches that keep coming back.

This page sums up published research reviews and guidelines. It is not a recommendation. Talk to a clinician before you start any treatment.

Order: lower risk first, then stronger evidence, then lower cost. Every treatment we have for this area is listed.

  1. Tinted or coloured lenses (e.g. FL-41) for light sensitivity

    Minimal risk$100 to $1,000

    Evidence: Very little or conflicting evidence.

    Studied in: People with brain injury (not only mild) and light sensitivity; mainly persistent symptoms; ages not given.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Glasses or contact lenses with a coloured filter, worn as needed. The reviews report no adverse effects, and no serious harm is plausible beyond the usual care needed with dark lenses when driving or in dim light.

    Cost: Assumes one pair of ready-made FL-41 tinted glasses (TheraSpecs FL-Pro frames listed at $99-159; prescription versions from about $180). A custom 'precision tint' fitting with an optometrist costs more but usually stays under $1,000.

    Sources: Bansal, 2022, NeuroRehabilitation; Barton, 2021, Survey of Ophthalmology

  2. Sub-symptom-threshold aerobic exercise

    Low risk$100 to $1,000

    Evidence: Stronger evidence (Moderate certainty (GRADE)).

    Studied in: Mostly teens and young adults after sport concussion, both in the first 2 weeks and with lasting symptoms.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: The exercise is set by a graded treadmill or bike test (e.g. the Buffalo Concussion Treadmill Test) to stay below the heart rate that brings on symptoms, and it is scaled back if symptoms worsen. Brief, self-limiting symptom flare-ups during testing and exercise are common and expected. Reviews report no serious adverse events: none were documented across 12 studies in post-concussion syndrome (McIntyre 2020, PMID 32079897), and there were no deaths or hospitalisations in 7 studies of acute mTBI, where one participant's symptoms worsened (Coman 2024, PMID 40959158). Leddy 2023 concluded that prescribed aerobic exercise in the first 2 weeks safely helps recovery. No serious harm specific to this treatment was found, so none is listed. General cardiac or musculoskeletal risks of exercise were not looked up and are not claimed.

    Cost: Reuses the existing EX-SSTAE profile. Assumes the trial protocols: about 6 physical-therapist contacts over 12 weeks (an exercise test, 2 supervised sessions and repeat tests at weeks 3, 6 and 9), with the exercise itself done at home. A shorter 6-week version uses 2 visits plus phone calls. US cash prices are about $100-200 per session at independent clinics ($200-350+ at hospital clinics) and $150-350 for a first evaluation. That makes roughly $650-1,350 for the 12-week course and $250-550 for the shorter one. The typical course falls in $100-1,000, though full hospital-based delivery can go over $1,000. A heart-rate monitor costs extra and was not priced.

    Sources: Vuu, 2022, NeuroRehabilitation; Leddy, 2023, British Journal of Sports Medicine; Langevin, 2020, Medicine and Science in Sports and Exercise

  3. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)

    Low riskOver $1,000

    Evidence: Limited evidence (Limited trial evidence).

    • Typical cost over $1,000 without insurance

    Studied in: Adults with lasting post-concussion symptoms.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: CBT is a talking therapy with no physical or pharmacological harm route. In the only included trial (McGeary 2022, PMID 35759281, PMC9237802; 8 sessions of CBT for headache vs cognitive processing therapy vs usual care in 193 veterans with posttraumatic headache and PTSD symptoms), adverse events were described as 'minimal and similar across treatment groups'. Most of the 160 adverse event records (changes in headache or musculoskeletal pain, increased stress, depression) were judged not study related. The only events attributed to participation were 2 temporary exacerbations of PTSD symptoms, both in the cognitive processing therapy arm, not the CBT-for-headache arm. Per-arm counts and serious adverse events appear only in a supplement (eTables 6-9) that was not available to us. Outside the trial, a meta-analysis of 56 RCTs of psychological interventions for adult PTSD (Hoppen 2022, PMID 35959698, DOI 10.1192/bjp.2022.111) found harms were generally rare (0-5%). Psychological treatment lowered the risk of deterioration compared with controls, and trauma-focused CBT had fewer serious adverse events than other psychological therapies. Rated 'low' rather than 'minimal' because psychotherapy can cause recognised but uncommon and usually temporary harms: symptom deterioration and short-lived increases in distress. No serious harm caused by CBT could be sourced, so serious_harms is left empty.

    Cost: Assumes the course as delivered in the trial: 8 one-hour individual sessions with a licensed psychologist, plus the intake assessment that is usual in practice. The unit price is the average US private-practice cash rate of $143.26 per session, from 175,083 psychotherapists in a national directory (Zhu 2024, PMID 39301411). 8 x $143 = about $1,150 before any intake fee, so the typical uninsured course falls in the >$1,000 band. The course sits close to the boundary: sliding-scale, community-clinic or lower-fee therapists ($100-125 per session) would bring it under $1,000, while doctoral psychologists in high-cost areas would push it well above. Psychotherapy is normally covered by US health insurance under mental health parity and by the VA for veterans. However, about one-third of private-practice psychotherapists do not accept insurance (Zhu 2024), so many patients still pay out of pocket.

    Sources: Sullivan, 2020, Disabil Rehabil; Chen, 2020, J Psychosom Res; Rytter, 2021, JAMA Netw Open

  4. Neck (cervical) physical therapy and manual therapy

    Low riskOver $1,000

    Evidence: Limited evidence (Limited trial evidence).

    • Typical cost over $1,000 without insurance

    Studied in: Teens and adults with neck pain, dizziness or headache lasting more than 10 days.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Minor, short-lived effects such as soreness, stiffness or headache after manual therapy are common: about 22% in RCTs and 41% in cohort studies, with major adverse events about 0.13% (Carnes 2010, PMID 20097115). The recognised serious harm of hands-on neck treatment is cervical artery dissection or stroke. It is very rare, and in a review of 334 published serious cases, 75% followed high-velocity manipulation rather than gentle mobilisation or exercise (Leung 2025, PMID 39663097). Serious harm is rare, so the risk is low for mobilisation and neck exercise. Thrust manipulation calls for vascular screening.

    Cost: Assumes a course of an initial evaluation plus about 6-8 physical therapy visits over 6-8 weeks, similar to cervicovestibular rehabilitation trials. Without insurance, US physical therapy typically costs $100-350+ per session (Hinge Health cost summary), so a typical course costs about $800-2,800. Most courses land above $1,000, though a short course at a low-cost clinic could come in under it. Insurance and Medicare usually cover PT, subject to copays or deductibles.

    Sources: Reid, 2022, Br J Sports Med; Schneider, 2023, Br J Sports Med (Amsterdam 2022 consensus review); Rytter, 2021, JAMA Netw Open (Danish guideline)

  5. Greater occipital nerve block (injection at the back of the head)

    Low risk$100 to $1,000

    Evidence: Very little or conflicting evidence.

    Studied in: Adults with hard-to-treat post-traumatic headache or occipital nerve pain; one teen case series

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: An injection of local anaesthetic, sometimes with a steroid, under the scalp at the back of the head. In headache care generally, peripheral nerve blocks are well tolerated; serious side effects such as acute cerebellar syndrome and infection are rare but reported (Stern 2022). In post-traumatic occipital neuralgia, the pilot RCT reported no serious adverse events (Stone 2024). Steroid-containing blocks can cause local hair loss (one of 15 teenagers in Seeger 2015). Brief injection pain, dizziness or fainting can occur. Serious harm is rare, so the risk is low.

    Cost: Assumes 1-3 blocks (CPT 64405), as blocks are often repeated because relief can be short-lived. The Medicare office rate is about $79; one cost summary estimates $155-290 all-in per injection and notes uninsured patients may be charged 150-500% of the Medicare rate (medical-costs-site CPT 64405 page). A course of 1-3 blocks is about $155-870; hospital-based or imaging-guided blocks cost more.

    Sources: Stone, 2024, Front Neurol (pilot RCT: PRP vs steroid/anaesthetic block vs saline); Conidi, 2016, Curr Pain Headache Rep (review of interventional treatment for post-traumatic headache); File, 2025, Pain Physician (retrospective chart review); Seeger, 2015, J Child Neurol (paediatric case series)

  6. Preventive medication for post-traumatic headache

    Moderate risk$100 to $1,000

    Evidence: Very little or conflicting evidence.

    • Moderate risk: Amitriptyline (tricyclic): boxed warning - increased suicidal thinking and behaviour in children, adolescents and young adults; Amitriptyline: arrhythmias, conduction prolongation; myocardial infarction and stroke reported with the class; can precipitate angle-closure glaucoma; Topiramate: acute myopia and secondary angle-closure glaucoma (possible permanent vision loss); metabolic acidosis; Topiramate: fetal toxicity (cleft lip/palate), suicidal ideation, cognitive/neuropsychiatric effects, serious skin reactions; Erenumab (anti-CGRP): constipation with serious complications (hospitalisation, surgery), new or worsening hypertension, hypersensitivity incl. anaphylaxis, Raynaud's phenomenon

    Studied in: Adults with headache after injury, including military groups. Drugs are borrowed from migraine care.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Prescription drugs that need a clinician to select, titrate and monitor them. Each class used (tricyclics such as amitriptyline, anticonvulsants such as topiramate, anti-CGRP antibodies such as erenumab) carries labelled serious harms: amitriptyline has a boxed warning for suicidality in children and young adults and can cause arrhythmias; topiramate can cause angle-closure glaucoma, metabolic acidosis, cognitive slowing and birth defects (needs pregnancy counselling and bicarbonate monitoring); erenumab can cause serious constipation, new or worse hypertension and hypersensitivity. Common minor effects (drowsiness, dry mouth, weight change, tingling) frequently lead to stopping; the 2026 review notes a high discontinuation rate for erenumab in PTH studies.

    Cost: Course assumption: a 3-month trial of a generic oral preventive (amitriptyline 25 mg nightly or topiramate 50 mg twice daily) with an initial and a follow-up prescriber visit. Drug cost is trivial (NADAC Oct 2026: amitriptyline 25 mg $0.05/tablet, topiramate 50 mg $0.04/tablet, i.e. under $10 for 3 months at acquisition cost; cash pharmacy prices are higher but typically well under $100). Two established-patient office visits (CPT 99214, 2026 Medicare non-facility 4.06 RVU x $33.40 = about $136 each) add about $270, so about $280-400 in total. Exception: an anti-CGRP antibody is >$1,000 - Aimovig NADAC is about $753 per monthly autoinjector, about $2,260 for 3 months without insurance.

    Sources: Saleh S, 2026, Cephalalgia; Ashina H, 2024, Cephalalgia (International Headache Society trial guidelines)

  7. Hyperbaric oxygen therapy

    Moderate riskOver $1,000

    Evidence: Very little or conflicting evidence.

    • Moderate risk: ear barotrauma in about 15% of patients, usually temporary; serious harms such as seizures are rare.
    • Typical cost over $1,000 without insurance

    Studied in: Adults, mostly with mild TBI.

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Treatment requires medical screening (ear equalisation, seizure history, lung disease) and chamber supervision. Middle-ear barotrauma is common (otologic adverse events in 15% of 18,284 patients across 54 studies; 6.4% of barotrauma episodes severe) and ear pain is a frequent reason to stop. Oxygen-toxicity seizures are serious but rare (about 0.05% of sessions in a 10,425-session registry), with higher risk in people with a seizure history. The 2026 meta-analysis reported most adverse events in TBI trials were mild and transient, and the 1.5 ATA dose used in concussion trials is lower than the 2.0-2.4 ATA in much of the harms data. The earlier pipeline profile rated this 'high' to match a rubric example; on this page's definitions (serious harm at a non-trivial rate, or supervision/screening needed) it fits 'moderate'.

    Cost: Course as used in concussion trials: about 40 sessions of 60 minutes at 1.5 ATA. US self-pay prices run from about $100 per session at freestanding clinics to over $1,000 at hospitals, so even the cheapest course (40 x $100 = $4,000) is well above $1,000; a typical $150-$250 per session gives $6,000-$10,000. Medicare NCD 20.29 does not list concussion/TBI as a covered indication, and commercial insurers generally follow similar lists, so this is usually paid out of pocket. Soft-sided 'mild' chambers (about 1.3 ATA) cost less but do not deliver the trial dose.

    Sources: Wang, 2026, Complement Ther Med; Crawford, 2017, J Head Trauma Rehabil; Harch, 2022, Front Neurol

Treatments for specific lasting symptoms

Some lasting symptoms, like migraine-type headache or poor sleep, also happen in other conditions. These treatments were studied mostly in people with those conditions, not after a concussion. Each one says what is known after concussion.

  1. Over-the-counter pain relievers (NSAIDs, acetaminophen) for headache attacks

    Low riskUnder $100

    Evidence in migraine and tension-type headache: Stronger evidence (Moderate to high strength of evidence (AHRQ) for NSAIDs; moderate for acetaminophen).

    Studied in: Adults with episodic migraine or tension-type headache, not specifically after concussion

    After concussion: One small open-label pilot trial in children with headache in the first days after concussion

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Used occasionally at label doses, side effects in trials were mild and transient (VanderPluym 2021) and no serious adverse events were reported in the Cochrane acetaminophen review. Serious harms are rare but recognised: NSAID stomach bleeding and (except aspirin) heart attack or stroke, liver damage from acetaminophen above 4,000 mg a day, and medication-overuse headache when used on too many days a month, which matters after concussion when headaches are frequent.

    Cost: Sold over the counter; a bottle of generic ibuprofen, naproxen or acetaminophen costs roughly $3-25 (GoodRx: ibuprofen average retail about $23 for the most common version, from $6.60; acetaminophen average retail about $6.52; naproxen similar), enough for many headache attacks.

    Sources: VanderPluym JH, 2021, JAMA; Stephens G, 2016, Cochrane Database Syst Rev; Diener HC, 2022, Neurol Res Pract (German S1 guideline on medication overuse headache)

  2. Anti-CGRP medicines to prevent migraine

    Low riskOver $1,000

    Evidence in migraine: Stronger evidence (Moderate to high quality of evidence (GRADE)). Needs a prescription.

    • Typical cost over $1,000 without insurance

    Studied in: People with episodic or chronic migraine, not specifically after concussion

    After concussion: Only small uncontrolled studies of erenumab in persistent post-traumatic headache

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Constipation and injection-site reactions are the common side effects and are usually mild; the labels warn of rare serious hypersensitivity (including anaphylaxis and angioedema), constipation with serious complications and hypertension. A 2026 meta-analysis of trials found no significant rise in hypertension risk, at very low certainty.

    Cost: Course assumed: monthly injections (or daily atogepant tablets) for at least 6-12 months. A US cost-effectiveness analysis used an annual erenumab drug price of $6,900; the other antibodies and gepants are priced similarly, so even a few months exceeds $1,000 without insurance.

    Sources: Sacco S, 2022, J Headache Pain (European Headache Federation guideline, 2022 update); Diener HC, 2026, Dtsch Arztebl Int (review summarising the International Headache Society guideline)

  3. Botulinum toxin injections (onabotulinumtoxinA) for chronic migraine

    Low riskOver $1,000

    Evidence in chronic migraine: Stronger evidence (Moderate certainty (GRADE)). Needs a prescription.

    • Typical cost over $1,000 without insurance

    Studied in: Adults with chronic migraine, not specifically after concussion

    After concussion: One small crossover trial (40 veterans) in post-traumatic headache

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Mostly minor, self-limiting effects (neck pain, injection-site pain, eyelid droop); the Cochrane review found adverse events in 60/100 treated vs 47/100 placebo, described as non-serious. The label carries a boxed warning for distant spread of toxin effect (swallowing or breathing difficulty), which is rare at chronic-migraine doses. Given by a trained clinician every 12 weeks.

    Cost: Course: 155 units every 12 weeks (label dose), usually from a 200-unit vial, for at least 2-3 treatment cycles. Medicare payment limit for J0585 is $6.497 per unit (Oct 2025 ASP file), i.e. about $1,000-1,300 drug cost per session before the injection procedure fee; cash prices are typically higher.

    Sources: Herd CP, 2018, Cochrane Database Syst Rev

  4. Behavioural treatments for headache (relaxation, biofeedback, CBT)

    Low riskOver $1,000

    Evidence in migraine and tension-type headache: Limited evidence (Low strength of evidence (SOE: low)).

    • Typical cost over $1,000 without insurance

    Studied in: Adults with migraine or tension-type headache, not specifically after concussion

    After concussion: A few trials in post-traumatic headache: one RCT in veterans found CBT reduced headache disability; a smaller Danish RCT found no effect

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Relaxation training, biofeedback and CBT are non-drug, non-invasive treatments with no plausible route to serious physical harm. In the largest post-traumatic headache trial (McGeary 2022, PMID 35759281) adverse events were 'minimal and similar across treatment groups'. A meta-analysis of CBT for migraine (Bae 2021, PMID 35056352) noted that two trials reported adverse effects including worsening of migraine intensity and frequency, respiratory symptoms and vivid memory of a traumatic event. Rated 'low' rather than 'minimal' because psychological therapy can cause uncommon, usually temporary symptom worsening or distress. No serious harm could be sourced, so serious_harms is empty.

    Cost: Reuses the existing CBT cost profile. Assumes a typical course of about 8 one-hour individual sessions with a psychologist (as in the McGeary 2022 CBT-for-headache trial, PMID 35759281). Average US private-practice cash rate is $143.26 per session (Zhu 2024, PMID 39301411), so 8 x $143 = about $1,150 before any intake fee. The course is close to the boundary: shorter courses, group formats or lower-fee therapists bring it under $1,000, and relaxation training learned from free or low-cost apps or recordings can cost under $100. Psychotherapy is usually covered by insurance, but about one-third of private-practice psychotherapists do not accept it.

    Sources: Treadwell JR, 2025, Headache (systematic review and meta-analysis, PMC11951403); Yuan L, 2025, J Headache Pain (systematic review and meta-analysis, PMC12763951)

  5. Amitriptyline to prevent migraine or chronic tension-type headache

    Moderate risk$100 to $1,000

    Evidence in migraine and chronic tension-type headache: Stronger evidence (Moderate certainty (GRADE), for migraine prevention). Needs a prescription.

    • Moderate risk: Adverse events leading to discontinuation more common than placebo (high certainty); Suicidal thinking and behaviour in children, adolescents and young adults (boxed warning); Arrhythmias, conduction delay and QT prolongation; myocardial infarction and stroke reported with tricyclics; Serotonin syndrome, especially with other serotonergic drugs; angle-closure glaucoma; dangerous in overdose

    Studied in: Adults with migraine or chronic tension-type headache, not after concussion

    After concussion: One small, inconclusive trial after mild TBI; otherwise only retrospective studies

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Side effects (drowsiness, dry mouth, weight gain, dizziness) are common: high-certainty evidence shows amitriptyline causes more people to stop because of adverse events than placebo. The label carries a boxed warning on suicidal thoughts in young people, and warns of heart rhythm problems, serotonin syndrome and angle-closure glaucoma; tricyclic overdose can be fatal. Needs a prescriber, screening for heart disease and drug interactions, and monitoring.

    Cost: Course assumption: about 6 months of low-dose generic amitriptyline plus two prescriber visits (start and follow-up/titration). Drug: GoodRx (accessed 2026-11-04) lists the most common version at $4.00 with a coupon (average retail $10.04), so about $25-60 for 6 months. Visits: CPT 99214, 2026 Medicare non-facility about 4.06 RVU x $33.40 = about $136 each, about $270 for two. Total about $300; the drug alone is under $100, but the required prescriber visits put the course in this band.

    Sources: Lampl C, 2023, J Headache Pain (systematic review and network meta-analysis, GRADE; European Headache Federation authors); Tao QF, 2026, Ann Med (systematic review and network meta-analysis, tension-type headache prophylaxis)

  6. Beta-blockers (propranolol, metoprolol) to prevent migraine

    Moderate risk$100 to $1,000

    Evidence in migraine: Stronger evidence (Moderate certainty (GRADE)). Needs a prescription.

    • Moderate risk: Contraindicated in bronchial asthma, sinus bradycardia, greater than first-degree heart block and cardiogenic shock (bronchospasm, dangerous slowing of the heart); Abrupt withdrawal can exacerbate angina and, in some cases, cause myocardial infarction; May mask signs of hypoglycemia in insulin-dependent diabetes; Increased adverse events leading to discontinuation versus placebo (moderate certainty)

    Studied in: Adults with migraine, not specifically after concussion

    After concussion: No concussion-specific trials found

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Side effects such as tiredness, dizziness and low heart rate or blood pressure are common, and the same review found moderate certainty evidence that beta-blockers increase adverse events leading to discontinuation. Serious harms are uncommon but screening is needed: the label lists asthma, sinus bradycardia and heart block as contraindications, warns that stopping abruptly can worsen angina or cause heart attack, and notes it can mask warning signs of low blood sugar in diabetes.

    Cost: Assumes generic propranolol 40 mg twice daily (60 tablets/month) for a 6-month preventive course. GoodRx lists an average retail price of $51.06 for 60 x 40 mg (about $306 for 6 months) and a discount-coupon price of $18.43 (about $110 for 6 months). Generic metoprolol is similarly inexpensive. A prescription is required, so a clinician visit adds to the cost.

    Sources: Lampl C, 2023, J Headache Pain (systematic review and network meta-analysis, 74 RCTs, GRADE)

  7. Topiramate to prevent migraine

    Moderate risk$100 to $1,000

    Evidence in migraine: Stronger evidence (High certainty (GRADE)). Needs a prescription.

    • Moderate risk: Acute myopia and secondary angle-closure glaucoma (possible permanent vision loss); metabolic acidosis; kidney stones; Fetal toxicity (cleft lip/palate), suicidal ideation, cognitive and neuropsychiatric effects; Adverse events leading to discontinuation: about 80 more per 1,000 than placebo

    Studied in: Adults with episodic or chronic migraine, not specifically after concussion

    After concussion: No randomised trials; one retrospective study in soldiers with post-traumatic headache suggested benefit

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Prescription drug that needs a clinician to start, titrate and monitor it. In the EHF meta-analysis (8 RCTs, 2,610 patients) about 80 more per 1,000 people stopped topiramate than placebo because of side effects; tingling, taste change, weight loss and cognitive slowing are common and dose-related (Cochrane 2013). The label lists recognised serious harms: acute myopia and angle-closure glaucoma, metabolic acidosis, kidney stones, suicidal ideation, and birth defects (oral clefts), so people who could become pregnant need counselling and contraception review.

    Cost: Reuses the existing headache-medication cost profile. Course assumption: a 3-month trial of generic topiramate 50 mg twice daily with an initial and a follow-up prescriber visit. The drug itself is cheap (NADAC Oct 2026: topiramate 50 mg about $0.04 per tablet, under $10 for 3 months at acquisition cost; cash pharmacy prices are higher but typically well under $100). Two office visits (CPT 99214, 2026 Medicare non-facility about $136 each) add about $270, so about $280-400 in total. The medication alone is under $100.

    Sources: Raffaelli, 2023, J Headache Pain (European Headache Federation critical reappraisal and meta-analysis); Linde, 2013, Cochrane Database Syst Rev

  8. Triptans for migraine attacks

    Moderate risk$100 to $1,000

    Evidence in migraine: Stronger evidence (Moderate to high strength of evidence (AHRQ)). Needs a prescription.

    • Moderate risk: Myocardial ischaemia/infarction and Prinzmetal's angina; arrhythmias; Cerebral or subarachnoid haemorrhage and stroke; gastrointestinal and peripheral vasospastic ischaemia; Serotonin syndrome (especially with SSRIs/SNRIs); Medication-overuse headache with frequent use; Ischaemic events in people with vascular risk factors: no significant association found (meta-analysis of 4 studies, 426,840 people)

    Studied in: Adults with episodic migraine, not specifically after concussion

    After concussion: One small uncontrolled pilot trial of sumatriptan in post-traumatic headache (15 analysed); no randomised trials

    Why this risk and cost

    Risk: Prescription drugs that need a clinician to screen for cardiovascular disease before use: triptans narrow blood vessels and are contraindicated in coronary, cerebrovascular or peripheral vascular disease and uncontrolled hypertension, and the label advises a cardiac evaluation in people with multiple cardiovascular risk factors. In trials, adverse events were mostly mild and transient (tingling, warmth, chest or throat tightness; VanderPluym 2021). Serious vascular events are rare, and a 2025 meta-analysis of 4 studies found no significant link between triptan use and ischaemic events in people with risk factors. Frequent use (10 or more days a month) can cause medication-overuse headache, a particular concern when headaches are already persistent after concussion.

    Cost: Course assumption: 3 months of generic oral sumatriptan (three 9-tablet packs, 27 tablets) plus one prescriber visit to prescribe and screen. Drug: GoodRx (accessed 2026-11-04) lists 9 tablets of 50 mg at $16.65 with a discount coupon (retail cash $55-160 for 9 tablets; 27 tablets of 50 mg $25.44 with coupon, $473 retail), so about $25-75 for the course with a discount card. One established-patient visit (CPT 99214, 2026 Medicare non-facility 4.06 RVU x $33.40 = about $136). Total about $160-210; it stays in this band even at retail cash prices. Nasal sprays and injections cost more (GoodRx $53-105 per carton or spray with coupon).

    Sources: VanderPluym JH, 2021, JAMA; Karlsson WK, 2024, BMJ
