How concussion affects the brain

This page explains what scientists currently think happens inside the brain during and after a concussion. For each part, we say what is well established, what is still debated, and what is not yet known.

The forces involved

A concussion is a brain injury linked to rapid movement of the brain. It can be caused by direct forces, such as a blow to the head, or by indirect forces, such as a hit elsewhere that makes the head move suddenly. Turning or twisting (rotational) movement of the head appears to be the main contributor to injury risk, because of the brain's particular mechanical properties and the way it sits inside the head.

Many researchers think that strain, meaning brain tissue being stretched and deformed, is the root cause of concussion. Exactly how the head's movement during an impact relates to concussion is still debated. In laboratory and computer-model tests, where a football helmet was struck had a large effect on how much different brain areas were strained. There is growing evidence that the amount of force needed to cause a concussion differs from person to person.

The chemical "energy crisis"

In most concussions, symptoms come from brain cells not working properly, rather than from damage you could see on a scan. The physical jolt sets off a chain of chemical events, often called the "neurometabolic cascade", that upsets the delicate balance inside brain cells. The timing of these changes is well described in animals, while research in humans is still growing.

  • Brain cells fire suddenly and release excitatory neurotransmitters (chemical messengers that switch other cells on, such as glutamate), and charged particles called ions shift in and out of cells.
  • In early rat studies, concussion caused a large outflow of potassium (a charged particle) from cells, probably linked to this widespread glutamate release.
  • Pumping the ions back into balance uses a lot of energy and changes how the brain takes up glucose (sugar, its main fuel).
  • Together, these changes create high energy demands and a period that scientists call a "metabolic crisis".
  • Some scientists think that problems with mitochondria (the parts of cells that make energy) are the main chemical explanation for many concussion symptoms.
  • After the first burst, glucose use can drop: in a rat study of mild injury, brain glucose uptake was lower from 3 hours to 5 days after injury.
  • In people, clear changes in brain glucose use have been found even when the person's level of consciousness (Glasgow Coma Scale score) was normal.
  • In one human brain-scan (PET) study that included mild to severe injuries, the period of reduced glucose use began to resolve about one month after injury.
  • The size and length of these changes depend on how severe the injury is, and milder injuries return to normal faster.
  • Researchers have linked these chemical changes to features of concussion such as migraine-like symptoms, thinking difficulties and vulnerability to a repeat injury.

Blood flow changes

Concussion may disturb how the brain controls its blood supply, how its blood vessels respond to change, how much oxygen reaches brain tissue, and the nervous system's control of the heart and blood vessels. Animal studies suggest that reduced blood flow to the brain is one of the longest-lasting changes after concussion. In college football players scanned with a type of MRI that measures blood flow (arterial spin labeling), blood flow was reduced after concussion and then recovered. Athletes who were slower to recover still had lower blood flow in one brain area one month after injury. In another study of young football players, blood flow was lower 8 days after concussion than in the first 24 hours. By day 8, their symptom and thinking-test scores had returned to pre-season levels.

Studies using ultrasound of the brain's arteries (transcranial Doppler) have reported reduced cerebrovascular reactivity, meaning the brain's blood vessels respond less well to changes such as carbon dioxide levels in the blood. A 2024 review found signs of impaired blood-flow control about 2 days after sport concussion, but how large these problems are and how quickly they recover was not clear. The same review found that symptoms tended to settle before blood-flow control did. In a study of teenagers, changes in how brain blood vessels responded to carbon dioxide were still present at the second scan, about four months after injury. In a rat study of a more severe injury, a mismatch between blood flow and the brain's energy use (called "uncoupling") was linked to how much nerve-fibre damage developed.

Stretched nerve fibres

Axons, the long, thin nerve fibres that carry signals between brain cells, appear especially vulnerable to the forces of a head injury. Injury to axons spread across the brain, called diffuse axonal injury, may be a key part of concussion. In laboratory experiments on nerve cells grown in a dish, rapid stretching broke microtubules, the tiny tubes inside axons that act like tracks for moving supplies. This interrupted transport along the axon and led to swelling and, in these experiments, breakdown of the fibre. Often only some tracks break, so supplies may still get through on the tracks that remain intact. Similar changes have been seen in human brain tissue examined after traumatic brain injury.

Standard CT and MRI scans usually cannot show these changes, because they are subtle or absent at the level these scans measure. Diffusion imaging (including DTI, an MRI method that tracks how water moves along nerve fibres) can pick up changes that standard scans miss, but results often differ between studies. Studies that follow people over time generally show at least partial return toward normal, often alongside improvement in day-to-day function. In one study, unusual white-matter areas on DTI were found in about half of people with mild brain injury, but also in about 1 in 8 people without an injury. In that study, these DTI findings were not linked to how many symptoms people reported.

Inflammation

Inflammation, the body's natural response to injury, is an important "second wave" response after a brain injury. Most of what is known about it comes from research on moderate and severe brain injury. After mild brain injury, blood tests suggest an inflammatory response in the body that can be measured from about 6 hours to 12 months after injury. However, differences between studies make it hard to draw firm conclusions.

Microglia are the brain's own immune cells, and they also help shape connections between nerve cells. After injury, microglia switch into an inflammatory mode, which may take them away from their normal jobs. Inflammation in the brain can be both helpful and harmful for recovery. In some cases, damping down inflammation may also slow the brain's own repair processes.

The blood-brain barrier is the protective lining of the brain's blood vessels that controls what passes from the blood into the brain, and how best to detect leaks in it after injury is still unresolved. In an animal model of concussion, microglia moved to areas where the barrier was disrupted and sealed them. In a human MRI study, some amateur American football players (who have many head impacts, not necessarily a diagnosed concussion) showed signs of a leakier barrier that were not seen in comparison groups.

Brain networks and the autonomic nervous system

Scientists think mild brain injury causes widespread changes in how the brain functions as a whole. A review of 66 brain-scan (resting-state fMRI) studies found a possible slight trend toward weaker whole-brain functional connectivity, meaning how closely different brain areas work together, within the first month after injury. Results varied a lot, even for the same brain area, partly because studies used different methods.

Heart rate variability, the natural beat-to-beat variation in heart rhythm, is closely tied to changes in the autonomic nervous system (the "automatic" system that controls heart rate and blood pressure) that can arise after concussion. At rest, the evidence on heart rhythm control after concussion is conflicting. During light, steady exercise, studies have found a higher heart rate and lower heart rate variability for up to 10 days after concussion. A 2024 analysis suggested heart rate variability can stay lower even after symptoms have recovered. However, only two of the six studies in that analysis found a difference.

Concussion commonly causes exercise intolerance, where physical effort brings on or worsens symptoms. Research supports the idea that changes in the autonomic nervous system are an important part of this. The exact mechanisms, and how they relate to symptoms and recovery, still need more study. Some researchers think exercise intolerance may also come from altered control of blood flow to the brain. In a treadmill study, people with lasting post-concussion symptoms had a "symptom-limited" response, stopping earlier than uninjured people because symptoms came on or increased.

Why some symptoms last

Most people recover, but some continue to have physical, thinking, emotional or behavioural symptoms. Why this happens is still poorly understood. Current thinking is that lasting symptoms depend on complex interactions between physical, psychological and social factors, often called the "biopsychosocial" model. Ongoing symptoms may reflect concussion-related changes that are slow to settle, other conditions such as migraine, low mood or anxiety, chronic pain, neck injury, vision problems or balance-system problems, or a combination of these.

Some experts describe lasting concussion symptoms as an "interface" condition that spans neurology, psychiatry and psychology. In this view, factors from before the injury and psychological stress interact with the injury itself to produce lasting symptoms. A newer "network" view suggests that symptoms can activate, amplify and reinforce each other, rather than all coming from one single hidden cause. How much physical damage to brain structure contributes to lasting symptoms remains unclear.

  • Balance system: problems with the vestibular system (the inner ear and brain system for balance, sense of position and eye-movement control) are common after mild brain injury.
  • Vision: in one concussion clinic, 69% of teenagers had a vision diagnosis, such as difficulty focusing, keeping both eyes lined up for close work (convergence insufficiency), or making quick, accurate eye movements.
  • Neck: neck pain at the first visit has been linked to a 2.6 to 6.4 times higher risk of lasting symptoms.
  • Concussion and whiplash (neck injury from the head being jolted) overlap in many ways, and no clear boundary between them has been defined.
  • Headache: the most common types of headache after concussion are migraine-like and tension-type-like.
  • Possible headache mechanisms include weaker pain control by the brain, chemical (metabolic) changes, inflammation and activation of the trigeminal nerve system (the main pain nerves of the head and face).
  • In children, the true mechanisms of post-concussion headache remain unclear.
  • Sleep: poorer sleep in the early stage of concussion has been linked to poorer long-term recovery.
  • Sleep and brain inflammation appear to affect each other in both directions: inflammation influences sleep, and poor sleep promotes inflammation.
  • Mood and stress: anxiety, low mood and emotional distress early after injury predict symptom burden and day-to-day function up to a year later.
  • In adults, early thinking symptoms, a history of anxiety, depression or sleep disorders, and signs such as loss of consciousness or memory loss at the time of injury were the factors most strongly linked to lasting symptoms.

A window of vulnerability

Animal and human studies support the idea that the brain is more vulnerable for a while after a concussion: a second blow before it has recovered makes the chemical changes inside cells worse. In rats given two mild injuries, the energy-related changes were greatest when the injuries were 3 days apart. When the two injuries were 5 days apart, the rats' brain chemistry looked similar to that of uninjured animals. Clinical and experimental evidence shows that the size and length of problems depend on the number of injuries and the time between them. Several studies suggest that these metabolic weak spots may help explain the window of vulnerability.

In a study of college football players, 11 of the 12 repeat concussions within the same season happened within 10 days of the first. The same study suggested that previous concussions may be linked to slower recovery. Several studies suggest the brain's physiology may take longer to recover than symptoms do, which supports a period of gradually increasing activity before full contact sport. Reports of "second impact syndrome", meaning severe brain swelling after a second hit during recovery, are rare, and some experts argue the swelling is unrelated to the first concussion.

Measuring the injury

Concussion is currently diagnosed through a clinical assessment, because standard brain scans are usually inconclusive. There is no objective blood marker or visible brain change that confirms a concussion, which makes it hard to diagnose with scans or other tests.

Two of the best-studied blood markers of brain injury are proteins called UCH-L1 and GFAP. A large emergency-department study of adults (ALERT-TBI) found that a combined GFAP and UCH-L1 blood test could help rule out the need for a CT scan. In that study, 6% of patients had an injury inside the skull visible on CT. The combined test was cleared by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for evaluating mild brain injury.

In a large study of college athletes, blood levels of GFAP, UCH-L1 and another protein, tau, rose soon after concussion compared with the athletes' own pre-season levels. However, the combined GFAP and UCH-L1 result only partly separated athletes with concussion from uninjured athletes in contact sports. The researchers concluded that, for now, these blood markers are research tools for understanding how concussion works. A review for the 2022 international consensus on sport concussion concluded that advanced brain scans, blood markers, genetic tests and newer technologies are valuable for research, but there is not yet enough evidence to recommend them in clinical practice. That review also supported the idea that the brain's physiological recovery may continue after symptoms have recovered.

What's still unknown

  • Recovery timeline: because studies measure different things in different ways, no single "physiological time window" for recovery can yet be defined.
  • Force threshold: a definite level of force that causes concussion has not been found.
  • Animals vs people: the timing of chemical changes is well understood in animals but only beginning to be described after human concussion.
  • Individual differences: genetic differences may help predict recovery and symptom severity. Whether genetic testing has any role after sport concussion is still unclear, because research is limited.
  • Sex differences: differences between female and male brains, for example in blood flow, glucose use, inflammation and connectivity, may add to differences in how concussion shows up.
  • Targeted care: researchers are exploring "endotypes", linking a person's lasting symptoms to specific biological mechanisms so that diagnosis and care can be more targeted.
  • Long-term links: studies are under way to better connect the early changes after concussion with possible longer-term processes in the brain.
  • Research quality: gaps in research so far leave uncertainty about the causes, outlook and treatment of lasting symptoms.
