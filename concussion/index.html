What is a concussion?

What scientists understand about concussion today, in plain words. Each section lists the research it comes from.

Want more detail? How concussion affects the brain explains what happens inside the brain, step by step.

What a concussion is

A concussion is a mild traumatic brain injury, sometimes called mild TBI. It usually happens when a blow or jolt makes the head speed up or stop suddenly. It causes a short-term change in how the brain works.

You do not have to be knocked out to have a concussion. Being dazed or confused, or having a gap in memory around the injury, can be enough for doctors to diagnose it.

Brain scans, like CT or MRI, are usually normal. That is because concussion is mainly a problem with how the brain works, not damage you can see on a scan. Doctors diagnose it mainly from what happened and from your symptoms.

What happens inside the brain

Right after the injury, brain cells release a rush of chemical messengers. Charged particles (ions) also move in and out of the cells in ways they normally would not.

To get back to normal, the cells need a lot of extra energy, so scientists call this an "energy crisis." At the same time, the way the brain uses sugar for fuel, and its blood flow, can change. The long nerve fibres that carry signals between brain cells (axons) may not work as well for a while.

These changes help explain early symptoms like headache and trouble thinking. In people, they can last for weeks.

Common symptoms

Symptoms are different for everyone, but they tend to fall into a few groups.

Many of these symptoms are also common in people who have not had a concussion. But in a large study, people with a brain injury reported more than twice as many symptoms as people without one.

  • Body: headache, dizziness, poor balance, tiredness, and being bothered by light or noise.
  • Thinking: trouble paying attention or remembering things.
  • Feelings: changes in mood or emotions.
  • Sleep: sleeping poorly or differently than usual.

How recovery usually goes

Most people feel better within days to a few weeks.

Having more, or stronger, symptoms early on is the most consistent sign that recovery may take longer.

  • In studies of athletes, symptoms went away after about 2 weeks (14 days) on average.
  • Among student athletes, 93% were fully back at school within 10 days, without new school supports.
  • In a large study of children, most improvement happened in the first 2 weeks for young children, and in the first 4 weeks for older children and teen boys.
  • In that study, teen girls tended to take longer.
  • Across many studies, it is not clear that age or sex really changes recovery time, because the results vary so much.

When symptoms last

For some people, symptoms last longer than a month. This is common: up to about 3 in 10 adults have persisting symptoms. In children, about 1 in 3 still have symptoms a few weeks later. In a large US study of trauma-hospital patients, symptoms kept easing over time. Even so, many still had some symptoms a year later.

Lasting symptoms often have more than one cause. Many of these problems can be treated. Things that can add to them include:

  • The inner-ear and eye systems for balance and vision (vestibular and ocular systems).
  • The neck ("cervicogenic" means coming from the neck).
  • The system that controls heart rate and blood pressure (the autonomic nervous system).
  • Poor sleep.
  • Headaches or low mood that start in the weeks after the injury.
  • Past migraines, or a past concussion that took over a week to heal.
  • Past anxiety, depression, or sleep problems.
  • Stress and life pressures (psychosocial factors).

What helps early on

Guidelines used to say to rest until all symptoms were gone. That advice has changed. Expert reviews now find that strict rest until symptoms go away does not help.

In one trial, teens and young adults rested strictly for 5 days, or rested 1 to 2 days and then slowly returned to activity. The strict-rest group reported more symptoms and got better more slowly.

Current evidence supports a short rest of a day or two, with less screen time, then a gradual return to light activity. In a large study of children, those who were active in the first week were less likely to still have symptoms 4 weeks later. That study shows a link, not proof, and trials are still needed.

Myths and facts

  • Myth: You have to be knocked out to have a concussion. Fact: Being dazed, confused, or having a memory gap can be enough.
  • Myth: A normal brain scan means there was no concussion. Fact: Scans are usually normal, because concussion changes how the brain works, not its structure.
  • Myth: Total rest until every symptom is gone is best. Fact: Strict rest until symptoms go away has not been shown to help.
  • Myth: Lasting symptoms are just imagined. Fact: People with a brain injury report more than twice as many symptoms as people without one.
  • Myth: Helmets prevent concussions. Fact: Helmets protect against injuries like skull fractures and cuts, but have not been shown to prevent concussion.

What scientists still don't know

Research has grown a lot, but results are often mixed.

  • There is no blood test or special brain scan ready for routine use; these are still research tools.
  • Lasting symptoms are still diagnosed mainly by asking about symptoms; no other test has been shown to be accurate enough.
  • The brain's own healing may take longer than it takes for symptoms to go away. What this means is not yet clear.
  • Doctors cannot yet predict well who will recover slowly; the best risk tools are only moderately accurate.
  • There are still few high-quality studies of treatments.
  • In former amateur athletes, research has not found a higher risk of later mental health or brain diseases.
  • Some studies of former professional athletes suggest a higher risk of conditions like dementia; better studies are needed to confirm this.
